Three of the twelve engineers I've mentored now lead frontend teams. The interesting part is what they all had in common — and it wasn't the most code written.

This post is the honest version of what I've learned: what actually grows engineers, what I got wrong, and why seniority is a behavior rather than a rank.

## Senior is a behavior, not a rank

Junior engineers ask "how do I do this?" Senior engineers ask "why are we doing this, and should we?" The title follows the questions, not the other way around. Seniority shows up as *judgment*: knowing which 20% of a problem matters and pushing back on the rest.

I've seen engineers with five years of experience behave like juniors and engineers with eighteen months behave like seniors. The differentiator was never years — it was the questions they asked. The juniors asked for instructions; the seniors asked for *constraints*: what's the goal, who's the user, what could go wrong, what are we optimizing for? Given a ticket, a junior builds the thing; a senior builds the right thing and, just as often, argues that the ticket itself is wrong.

The practical test I use: give someone a vague problem and watch what they do. Do they come back with a plan and clarifying questions, or with a guess and a commit? The latter isn't a character flaw — it's an unlearned skill, and it can be taught.

## Write for the next reader

The biggest lever in a codebase is how the last person left it. Mentoring is teaching people to write code that a stranger in six months can read cold — clear names, small functions, comments that say *why* not *what*. I've watched a team's review cycle halve just from this habit.

Here's the thing about code readability: it's not about being nice — it's about *throughput*. Every time someone has to decode an unclear name, unravel a 300-line function, or reverse-engineer the intent behind a clever one-liner, the team pays a compounding tax. Code is read ten times more than it's written. Writing for the reader is the highest-ROI habit in software, and it's the first thing I teach.

The concrete habits that moved the needle:

- **Names are the documentation.** A variable called `users` in one place and `u` in another is a bug report waiting to happen. If a name needs a comment to be understood, the name is wrong.
- **Small functions with one job.** If you can't describe what a function does in one sentence without using "and," it does too much.
- **Comments explain why, never what.** The what is in the code; the why — "this handles the edge case where the API returns null on a 204" — is the part that disappears from collective memory.
- **The diff is a writing exercise.** A review that reads like a story — commit in the order of understanding, with each step leaving the code in a working state — is a gift to the reviewer and the future debugger.

I've watched a team's review cycle halve just from these habits. Not because reviews got shorter, but because there was less to argue about: the code was already legible, so the conversation could be about substance instead of style.

## Teach the uncomfortable review

Code review isn't a gate, it's a conversation. The engineers who grew fastest were the ones who learned to give feedback that is specific, kind, and about the code — and to receive it without taking it personally.

The hardest skill in engineering isn't writing code; it's *having your code discussed*. New engineers read "can we simplify this loop?" as "you're a bad engineer." The growth happened when we reframed review as collaboration — the reviewer's job is to make the code better, and the author's job is to defend decisions, not defend ego.

Equally important is teaching people to *give* feedback well. The pattern that works: state the observation (what the code does), state the impact (what it means for the reader or the runtime), and propose or request the fix. No character assassination, no rhetorical questions. Feedback is a craft, and like every craft, it improves with practice and honest calibration.

## Give away the interesting work

If you want a team to grow, the senior's job is to hand over the interesting, scary work — the big refactor, the new stack — and be there to catch. Every engineer I've seen plateau was being protected from hard problems. Growth is uncomfortable by design.

This is the lesson I learned last and the one I most wish I'd learned first. Early in my mentoring career, I did the thing well-meaning seniors do: I kept the hard problems for myself and gave my mentees the safe, well-scoped work. The team was calm, the seniors were busy, and the juniors were... not growing. When I finally handed over the scary migration — with the explicit promise that it was *theirs* to own, bugs and all — the transformation was immediate. They cared more, learned more, and produced more than they ever had on safe tickets.

The senior's job in that handoff is to be a safety net without being a crutch: available for questions, clear about decisions that are theirs to make, and honest about the parts that are genuinely hard. And then — this is the hard part — to *let them do it*, even when you can see a better way, because the learning is in the struggle, not the solution.

Seniority is a gift you give the people around you. The code is just the receipt. If you're a senior engineer, the most valuable thing you produce isn't your feature work — it's the engineers who outgrow needing you.
