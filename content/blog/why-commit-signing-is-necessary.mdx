In Git, when you push code to origin (a.k.a. the remote server), it uses your email address and name to distinguish between commits made by individual users, out of the box. So you can identify the commits made by you or your coworker by simply checking `git log`.

A simple `git log` would output something like this:

![Git log!](/blog/why-commit-signing-is-necessary/git-log.webp)

As you can see, a name and email under the author tag is associated with every commit hash.

## How spoofing works

Since this info is readily available to anyone who can view the repo, anyone can mimic a user by simply changing their own Git config file and adding the target's name and email address. If that person has permission to push changes to the repo, all their commits from then on would look like they were made by the target. That's where **commit signing** comes into play, and helps mitigate this.

## How signing works

Signing a commit involves using your private key to create a unique digital fingerprint, or signature, for that commit. This signature is like a stamp that only your private key can create. When you share your public key with platforms such as GitHub, they can use it to verify the signature on your commits. This verification process ensures that the commits you claim to have made actually come from you, adding a layer of security and trust to the collaboration process. It helps others know that the code changes or contributions attributed to you are authentic and haven't been tampered with.

## Conclusion

Without signing, anyone with push access can impersonate you. With signing, every commit has a cryptographic proof of authorship. Set it up once, and your team stops wondering who actually wrote what.

For a step-by-step guide, see GitHub's docs on [signing commits](https://docs.github.com/en/authentication/managing-commit-signature-verification/signing-commits).
