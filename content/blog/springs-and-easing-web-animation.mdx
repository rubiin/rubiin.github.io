Every interaction on a good site has a *feel*. Most of that feel comes from motion curves. You can have pixel-perfect layouts, impeccable type, and a flawless color system — and still lose the room the moment an element animates with the wrong timing. This post is about why spring-based motion feels better than fixed-duration easing, and the math underneath it.

## The problem with durations

A 300ms cubic-bezier is a promise: "this will take exactly 300ms." But physics doesn't work that way. When you swipe to dismiss a card, the distance you swiped should determine how far it travels — a small flick should travel less and settle sooner than a big throw. A fixed duration ignores that entirely.

Worse, fixed durations are tuned by eyeballing. The designer picks a curve that looks right in a demo, ships it, and the developer tweaks the number until it *feels* acceptable. Every screen has different distances, so the same 300ms that feels snappy on a 12px tooltip feels sluggish on a 240px modal. You end up with a matrix of magic numbers and nobody understands why any of them are what they are.

## What a spring actually is

A spring model is a physical system: a mass attached to a point, with stiffness pulling it back and damping resisting its velocity. The equation of motion is

$$
m \ddot{x} + c \dot{x} + k x = 0
$$

Where $m$ is mass, $c$ is damping, and $k$ is stiffness. The solution is a decaying oscillation — and when $c$ is critically damped, you get the fastest return without overshoot:

$$
x(t) = (A + Bt) e^{-\omega_0 t}
$$

Critically damped means the mass returns to rest as quickly as possible without bouncing past. Underdamped means it overshoots (that's the satisfying little bounce on a macOS window). Overdamped means it crawls — great for heavy, deliberate objects, wrong for anything that should feel light.

What matters for UI is that a spring is *distance-aware*. Throw a card across the screen and the spring travels far and takes proportionally longer; nudge it and it settles in a frame or two. The system is coherent without anyone tuning per-distance timings.

## Tuning knobs, not timelines

The great insight of spring physics for UI is that you tune *stiffness* and *damping* — physical properties — instead of durations and curves. A stiffer spring is faster; more damping kills bounce. Same mental model for a door, a card, a button.

In Motion, a spring is configured with `stiffness`, `damping`, and `mass` (the mass just scales how the other two feel):

```tsx
import { motion } from 'motion/react'

<motion.div
  animate={{ x: 200 }}
  transition={{ type: 'spring', stiffness: 300, damping: 30 }}
/>
```

The reason this wins in a team setting is *shareability*. One designer defines three springs — "snappy", "standard", "gentle" — and every screen in the product uses exactly one of them. The tooltip uses snappy, the modal uses standard, the full-screen hero uses gentle. Consistency falls out of the system instead of requiring discipline.

## When springs are wrong

Springs are not a universal answer. For anything that should feel *mechanical* — a progress bar, a scroll snap, a deterministic sequence — a spring's distance-awareness is a bug, not a feature. A progress bar that varies its duration based on some internal distance model would be confusing. Fixed easings exist for a reason, and the best systems mix both deliberately: springs for anything physical-feeling (drag, enter, exit), curated easings for anything instrumental (progress, scrub, state-driven).

## Practical rules I ship with

1. **Springs for enter/exit and drag; fixed easings for subtle fades.** Fades are cheap and nearly always fine with a 150–200ms ease-out. Movement with a spatial component deserves a spring.
2. **Never animate `top`/`left` — transform only.** Layout properties force reflow on every frame. `transform` and `opacity` are compositor-friendly and stay smooth.
3. **Respect `prefers-reduced-motion` by rendering the final state instantly.** Motion provides `useReducedMotion`; use it to skip the animation entirely rather than playing a slowed-down version. Some users have vestibular conditions — a slowed animation can be worse than none.
4. **Stagger, don't overlap.** When a list of items appears, stagger them by 40–60ms. Simultaneous animation reads as a wall; staggering reads as a wave.
5. **Keep it under 500ms.** A spring that takes longer feels like the UI is apologizing. If your spring needs more time, it's probably too far a travel — move less.

Motion in a UI is a *language*. Learn its grammar once and every product you build speaks it fluently — and your users will feel the difference even if they can't name it.

## The feel is the feature

The most underrated lesson from years of animation work: users forgive a lot of visual imperfection, but they *notice* when motion feels wrong — a janky scroll, a laggy hover, an over-eager bounce. Smooth motion is a trust signal, the same way a fast site is. The physics is worth learning because the payoff isn't just aesthetics; it's the quiet, unquantifiable sense that a product was made with care.
